What you are looking at

Why the count is Borel

One lane, no overtaking, and one point on the road where a car has to slow down. That is the entire model, and it is enough to produce an exact, named distribution.

A car reaches the disturbance and takes D seconds to get through it — the clearance time. For those D seconds nothing behind it can pass, so every car that arrives during the window has to stop as well. Each of those cars then takes its own D seconds, catching whoever arrives during its window, and so on. The cascade ends at the moment the queue empties.

Now count arrivals. If cars pass the point at a rate q per second, the number arriving during one D-second window is Poisson with mean

μ = q × D

Consecutive windows do not overlap, so those counts are independent. Draw a node for each car caught, joined to the car whose window caught it, and you have a Galton–Watson tree with Poisson(μ) offspring — that is the shape being drawn in the infield as the cascade runs. The total number of cars in the cascade is the total progeny of that tree, and total progeny of a Poisson branching process is exactly the Borel distribution:

P(N = n) = e−μn (μn)n−1 / n!

μ is the expected number of cars one braking car forces to brake, so it decides everything. Below 1 each car replaces itself less than once over and the cascade dies out, with mean size 1/(1 − μ) — that is the number on the gantry. At exactly 1 every cascade still ends, but the mean size is infinite: the tail falls off only as n−3/2, so arbitrarily large jams stay common. Above 1 a cascade survives forever with probability 1 − q, and the histogram switches to the law for the ones that do clear.

One lane is the point, not a shortcut

It is worth being clear about this, because the repository used to suggest otherwise: a single lane is exactly the setting in which the answer is Borel. What is being simulated is an M/D/1 queue — Poisson arrivals, deterministic service, one server — and the number of cars served in one busy period of an M/D/1 queue is Borel(μ). That is a theorem, and it needs one lane, not several.

Adding lanes would not make the model more truly Borel; it would break it. A braking car could then trigger cars beside it as well as behind, the number it catches would stop being Poisson, and the total would stop being Borel. That is a legitimate and interesting model — it is just a different one, and it has no closed form this tidy. The older note in this repository listing multiple lanes as the route to a “true” Borel had it backwards, and has been corrected.

What is exact here and what is not

The circuit is a genuine simulation, not an animation of a precomputed answer. A car is caught if it would reach the disturbance while the server is still working — the definition of a busy period — and the tree records which car’s clearance window caught it. Each cascade’s size is counted as it happens. Measured over three thousand cascades, the number of cars the first braker catches comes out Poisson to three decimal places (mean 0.699 against μ = 0.700), and P(N = n) tracks Borel across the small sizes. The mechanism is the real one.

Two things are idealised, and it is worth knowing which. The queue is a vertical queue: it is drawn stretching back along the road so the tailback is legible, but as far as the arithmetic is concerned it occupies no length. Letting it occupy real length would mean cars were caught the moment the tail reached them rather than when they reached the disturbance, which is a different and larger number — it lifts an intended μ of 0.70 above 1, and the cascades stop ending. Second, a car released from the queue is behind where the undisturbed traffic would have put it, and closes that gap at a little over cruising speed, which is what real traffic does leaving a jam.

One thing that surprises people: cars turn amber a long way back. A cascade’s fate is settled the moment it starts — whether a car gets caught depends only on whether it would reach the disturbance before the queue drains, and that is decided by where it already is. So a car is drawn amber from the instant the cascade has claimed it, which can be most of a minute before it arrives, and red once it is actually stopped. The amber stretch is the reach of the cascade made visible.

The genuine limitation is that the loop holds a fixed number of cars. Borel has a very long tail — its mean is dominated by rare enormous cascades — and a loop of 45 cars simply cannot host a cascade of 200. So the circuit’s cascades come out about a tenth smaller in the mean than the theory, entirely through the missing tail; the common sizes are right. That is why the histogram samples the idealised process separately, with no cap on how many cars can be caught, and why it is the one to trust for the shape of the law.

Things worth trying

Raise clearance time until μ passes 1 and watch the queue stop draining. Hold μ fixed but trade cars against circuit length — the distribution should not move, because only the product q × D matters. Switch to log scale near μ = 1 to see the straight-line n−3/2 tail. Then push the density high and watch the simulated cascades on the circuit pull away from the theory, which is the closed loop asserting itself.
